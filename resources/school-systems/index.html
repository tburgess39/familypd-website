Research-informed family engagement

Strong family-school partnerships require information, relationships, and shared capacity.

Effective family engagement is more than attending events. Families and educators need the knowledge, confidence, relationships, and opportunities to work together around student learning and development (Mapp & Kuttner, 2013).

Who does what?

Public education is a layered system.

1

Federal

Federal laws and agencies address civil rights, disability protections, student privacy, program funding, and national guidance.

2

State

State agencies set academic standards, testing requirements, educator rules, diploma requirements, and statewide policies.

3

District

Districts manage schools, calendars, transportation, course offerings, grading practices, staffing, programs, and local procedures.

4

School

Schools implement district and state expectations through schedules, instruction, supports, communication, and daily decisions.

When information conflicts, verify the current policy with the official district or state source and ask the school to explain how it applies to the student.

Common school terms

Know what the information is telling you.

Formative assessment

Practice and feedback used during learning to identify what a student understands and what should happen next. Strong formative assessment uses evidence to adjust teaching and learning (Black & Wiliam, 1998).

Summative assessment

An assessment used after a period of instruction to evaluate what a student has learned. Examples may include unit exams, final projects, end-of-course tests, or state assessments.

Weighted and unweighted GPA

An unweighted GPA generally reflects grades on a standard scale. A weighted GPA may add value for advanced courses. The exact calculation and maximum vary by district and state.

Credits and graduation requirements

High-school students must earn specific credits and meet state or district requirements. Families should review the transcript and graduation plan with a counselor at least annually.

Credit recovery

A way to regain credit after failing a course. It may not replace the original grade or meet every college, scholarship, athletic, or program requirement. Ask how it appears on the transcript.

Chronic absenteeism

Commonly defined as missing at least 10% of school days for any reason. Missing instruction can affect achievement, connection, and well-being; barriers should be addressed early with the school (U.S. Department of Education, 2025).

IEP

An Individualized Education Program under IDEA documents eligible special-education needs, goals, services, supports, placement, and progress measurement.

Section 504 plan

A plan under a federal civil-rights law that may provide aids, services, or accommodations so a qualified student with a disability has equal access to education (U.S. Department of Education, 2026).

FERPA

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act gives parents—and eligible students—rights involving access to and privacy of education records (Student Privacy Policy Office, 2021).

CTE and dual enrollment

Career and Technical Education connects academic, technical, and employability skills. Dual enrollment allows eligible students to complete college-level coursework while in high school; rules and costs vary.

Family monitoring system

Check the right information before it becomes an emergency.

Weekly

  • Attendance and tardies
  • Missing assignments
  • Current grades and teacher feedback
  • District and school messages

Each grading period

  • Course performance and trends
  • Assessment results
  • Supports, interventions, and accommodations
  • Goals for the next period

At least annually

  • Course requests and placement
  • Credits, transcript, and graduation progress
  • CTE, advanced coursework, dual enrollment, and career options
  • IEP or 504 review when applicable

Keep organized

  • Requests and follow-up emails
  • Plans, evaluations, and notices
  • Work samples and progress reports
  • Questions for the next meeting

Before a school meeting

Prepare for a productive conversation.

  1. What is the specific concern, with examples and dates?
  2. What information has the school already collected?
  3. What does the current policy or plan require?
  4. Who is responsible for the next action?
  5. When and how will progress be reviewed?

Family challenge

Create one school-information routine.

Choose a day to review the portal, calendar, messages, attendance, and upcoming deadlines. Write down one question and one celebration before closing the check-in.
