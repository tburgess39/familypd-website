Pillar Three

Education & Skills

Use learning as a bridge to confidence, options, and opportunity.

Education includes school, credentials, career preparation, digital literacy, practical life skills, and lifelong learning for every person in the household.

Start a family check-inReview sources

What research suggests

Use information to guide action—not to judge the family.

Family-school partnerships are strongest when both families and educators have the information and capacity to work together. Formative feedback can improve learning, and research on CTE has found positive associations with on-time graduation and postsecondary participation in some settings (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Brodersen et al., 2021; Mapp & Kuttner, 2013).

What this pillar includes

A practical whole-family view.

School navigation

Grades, attendance, assessments, credits, graduation requirements, services, and advocacy.

Learning habits

Curiosity, reading, practice, feedback, organization, and asking for help.

Career preparation

CTE, credentials, work-based learning, college, apprenticeships, and workforce programs.

Digital and life skills

Cybersecurity, AI literacy, communication, problem-solving, research, and everyday independence.

Notice the pattern

Signs this pillar may need attention

These are discussion signals, not diagnoses or proof that a family is failing.

  • The family does not know how to access grades, attendance, assignments, or school messages.
  • A student’s missing credits, low grades, or attendance problems are discovered late.
  • Family members view education as something that ends with school.
  • Career choices are being made without exploring skills, credentials, costs, or local opportunities.
  • People avoid asking questions because school or training language feels intimidating.

FamilyPD perspective

Begin with self-awareness. Choose one system. Clarify one policy. Reach for community support when the family needs knowledge, services, or accountability.

Build a family system

Small practices that make the pillar visible.

Choose what fits your household. A useful system is simple enough to repeat and flexible enough to improve.

1

Weekly school check

Review grades, attendance, assignments, and messages before problems become emergencies.

2

Shared learning calendar

Track tests, projects, registration windows, applications, and credential deadlines.

3

Question-first routine

Normalize “I do not know yet” and identify where to verify the answer.

4

Learning portfolio

Save strong work, credentials, reflections, projects, resumes, and evidence of growth.

5

Career comparison sheet

Compare job duties, education, cost, time, salary ranges, and local training options.

6

Teach-back habit

After learning something, explain it to another family member in plain language.

Family meeting

Talk about it together.

  1. What is each person learning right now—in or outside school?
  2. What school or training information do we need to verify?
  3. Are grades showing mastery, missing work, attendance issues, or another barrier?
  4. Which career-preparation option best matches interests, time, cost, and goals?
  5. What skill could one family member teach another?

Use age-appropriate language. People may pass, ask for time, or share privately when a topic feels sensitive.

Family challenge

Turn insight into one repeatable action.

Choose one learning system: a weekly portal check, a shared reading period, a family teach-back, a career comparison, or a small project that produces something the learner can show.
